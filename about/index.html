About this catalogue

What it is, how it was built, what it is not, and who maintains it.

Why it exists

Pakistan ran an unusual number of large public programmes that reached different places at different times. Health insurance activated division by division across Punjab inside twelve months. Land records digitised tehsil by tehsil across 143 centres. Cash transfers were gated by the date each district's poverty census finished, and payments began in some districts before the census had ended in others. Districts were created, merged and renamed throughout.

That is the variation causal inference needs, and most of it has never been used. The reason is rarely that the question is uninteresting. It is that establishing when each district was treated is a research project in itself, and there is no way to know in advance whether an outcome exists at the same resolution. Both facts sit in scattered agency reports, project documents and evaluation appendices.

This catalogue collects them in one place, for 67 programmes and natural experiments, with 322 programme–outcome pairs and 55 datasets mapped against them — and grades how reliable the timing data actually is, including where the answer is that it is not reliable at all.

How it was built

Entries were assembled from agency websites and annual reports, provincial Annual Development Programme scheme registers, World Bank and ADB project appraisal and implementation documents, third-party evaluations, and the published academic literature. Where an evaluation had already assembled a district rollout table — as Malik, van de Poel and van Doorslaer did for health-unit contracting across 75 districts — the catalogue points at it rather than duplicating it.

Each entry carries the source for its timing data and an assessment of how usable that source is, on a four-point scale documented on the timing page. Of the 67 entries, 26 are graded High and 19 Medium-High. The remainder are included deliberately: either because the gap is worth knowing about, or so that you can rule them out quickly.

What it is not

It is not a census of Pakistani public policy. Coverage is strongest for Punjab, which publishes the most, and thinnest for Balochistan, which publishes the least and is also missing from the district-representative samples of several national surveys. The density of entries reflects what is documented, not what happened.

It is not a repository of treatment-timing data. It tells you where that data can be found; it does not host it, except where contributors have supplied files.

It is not evidence. The expected signs attached to each programme–outcome pair are hypotheses derived from the stated mechanism, several of them deliberately ambiguous. Nothing here has been estimated.

Reusing it

Everything is released under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 licence. Take the CSVs, build on them, fork the site. The one thing worth asking: if you correct something, send the correction back, so the next person inherits the fixed version rather than repeating the error.
